# Denver's curb ramps: density peaks where pedestrian demand is middling

**One-line finding:** Across Denver's 479 pedestrian-demand zones, the zones the city rates highest for walking demand (Pedestrian Demand Index HIGH, scores 7-10) hold 563.3 curb ramps per square mile, fewer than the medium-demand zones at 663.1 per square mile, out of 61,983 ramps placed inside the zones.

Source: City and County of Denver open data, curb ramp layer (ODC_TRANS_CURBRAMPS_P, 72,770 records pulled October 28, 2026) joined to the city's Pedestrian Demand Index layer (ODC_ADMN_PEDDEMANDINDEX_A, 479 zones). Crash figures join both layers to Denver crash reports from January 1, 2021 to October 15, 2026, with the method and its limits in Finding 6 and the data notes.

Two caveats govern everything below, and they are stated here rather than in a footnote. First, a curb ramp point in this layer is a location delineated from aerial imagery: the service description states that ramps do not have to be ADA accessible to be captured, because imagery resolution cannot discern accessibility. No figure in this brief is a finding about ADA compliance, ramp condition, or crossing quality. Second, the Pedestrian Demand Index is a demand proxy built from population and employment density (Finding 3), not a count of pedestrians. Comparisons against it are comparisons against estimated demand, not measured walking volumes.

## Finding 1: The ramp layer holds 72,770 points, captured from aerial imagery in cycles through 2022

The curb ramp layer (TRANS_CURBRAMPS_P) holds 72,770 point records, each with a map point, pulled in full on October 28, 2026 (the server count and the local count are equal). The service description states the points were delineated from 2022 aerial imagery (DRCOG Denver Regional Aerial Photography Project), in a dataset first delineated from 2014 imagery and updated in 2016, 2018, 2020, and 2022. The layer's service data was last edited July 12, 2024.

Each record carries six fields besides its object ID: a creation date, a creator name, a comments field, an update status, and a unique ID. Their population is uneven:

- UPDATE_STATUS is populated on all 72,770 records, with two of its three domain values in use: "NC" (No Change) on 69,986 records (96.2 percent) and "A" (Add) on 2,784 (3.8 percent). No record carries "M" (Modify). The status is relative to the previous capture cycle: it records that 2,784 points were additions in the cycle that produced this dataset and 69,986 were unchanged, not that 2,784 ramps were built in any single year.
- CREATEUSER is the same value ("Kucera") on all 72,770 records.
- COMMENTS is null on all 72,770 records, so the layer carries no per-ramp notes.
- UNIQUE_ID is present on all 72,770 records, with no duplicates.

Without the join in Finding 4, this layer can say where ramps were seen from the air and nothing about where walking demand is concentrated; the demand layer is what makes the comparison in Finding 5 possible.

## Finding 2: The layer's only date is a delineation date, not a construction date

CREATEDATE is populated on all 72,770 records, and its values run from July 8, 2015 to February 5, 2024. The distribution clusters in five years: 54,120 records (74.4 percent) carry a 2015 date, 7,391 carry 2017, 5,527 carry 2019, 2,854 carry 2021, and 2,770 carry 2023, with small tails in 2016 (10), 2022 (84), and 2024 (14).

These are the dates records were delineated and created in the GIS during and after each imagery capture cycle, not dates the physical ramps were built: a ramp network built over many decades does not acquire three quarters of its records in a single year. This brief therefore computes no ramp age, no installation trend, and no "new ramp" count from CREATEDATE, and no figure in it treats the date as construction timing. The clustering is consistent with the service description's capture cycles (imagery from 2014, 2016, 2018, 2020, and 2022, with delineation recorded in the following years), and that is as far as the record supports reading it.

## Finding 3: The Pedestrian Demand Index rates 479 zones on a 1-10 quantile scale

The Pedestrian Demand Index layer (ADMN_PEDDEMANDINDEX_A) holds 479 polygon records, one per Traffic Analysis Zone, covering 154.39 square miles in total. Per its service description, the index adds population density (2020 Census) to employment density (LEHD LODES, Version 7), groups the combined density into 10 groupings by the quantile method, and assigns each zone a score from 1 (lowest estimated demand for walking) to 10 (highest). The description also notes the results should be considered estimates, because of geographic problems in the base units used to combine the two densities.

The score distribution is the quantile design made visible: each score from 1 to 9 holds exactly 48 zones, and score 10 holds 47. The layer's own PDI_Ranking field bands the scores as LOW (scores 1-4, 192 zones), MEDIUM (scores 5-6, 96 zones), and HIGH (scores 7-10, 191 zones); this brief uses the layer's own bands and states the score range wherever a band is named. Zone areas vary widely within the scale: score-1 zones cover 59.03 square miles in total (including the two largest zones in the city, Finding 5), while the 47 score-10 zones cover 0.63 square miles combined. The layer's service data was last edited August 7, 2026, as reported by the service.

## Finding 4: 61,983 of 72,770 ramps fall inside the demand zones; the rest sit outside the city

Point-in-polygon assignment places 61,983 of the 72,770 ramp points (85.2 percent) inside a PDI zone. The remaining 10,787 points (14.8 percent) fall outside every PDI polygon. That unmatched share is above the 5 percent flag line in this project's methods, so its composition matters, and the record supports a specific explanation: the ramp layer was captured from regional aerial imagery and extends beyond the City and County of Denver (its points run west to longitude -105.366 and south to latitude 39.543, outside the city's boundary), while the PDI zones cover the city only.

A second, independent polygon set checks the explanation. Assigning the same points against the city's 78 statistical neighborhood polygons places 61,999 ramps (85.2 percent) inside the city. The two joins disagree on only 30 ramps in total (23 fall inside a neighborhood but outside every PDI zone, and 7 the reverse), so both polygon sets place the same set of points inside Denver and the same roughly 10,800 points outside it. The unmatched ramps are therefore ramps outside the city boundary, not points the join failed to place inside it. Every density figure below uses the 61,983 placed ramps as its numerator population and states so.

The crash join in Finding 6 has a different match profile: 100,085 of 101,770 geocoded crash reports (98.3 percent) fall inside a PDI zone.

## Finding 5: Ramp density is highest in medium-demand zones, not high-demand zones

Dividing each band's placed ramps by that band's zone area (ramps divided by square miles of the same zones, on both sides of every rate):

- LOW (scores 1-4): 30,244 ramps over 102.15 square miles = 296.1 ramps per square mile.
- MEDIUM (scores 5-6): 15,334 ramps over 23.13 square miles = 663.1 ramps per square mile.
- HIGH (scores 7-10): 16,405 ramps over 29.12 square miles = 563.3 ramps per square mile.

The area-weighted density peaks in the middle of the demand scale and is lower where demand is rated highest. The HIGH band holds 26.5 percent of the placed ramps (16,405 of 61,983) on 18.9 percent of the zoned area (29.12 of 154.39 square miles). Using the narrower top band a reader might expect, scores 8-10 only, gives 8,815 ramps over 16.52 square miles = 533.6 per square mile, the same direction.

By individual score, density rises from 134.9 per square mile at score 1 to a peak of 679.2 at score 5, then declines through score 9 (499.9), with score 10 highest at 864.9. Score 10 is the control this package built in: its 47 zones are the smallest and densest-demand zones in the city (0.63 square miles combined, the downtown core), and they do show the highest density of any score, which is the expected pattern if the join and the area arithmetic are sound.

Two qualifications are part of this finding, not additions to it. First, the median zone tells a different story from the area-weighted band total: the median HIGH zone holds 714.0 ramps per square mile, against 660.2 for the median MEDIUM zone and 479.5 for the median LOW zone. The band totals are pulled down by large, low-density zones inside the HIGH band: 27 of the 191 HIGH zones (14.1 percent) sit below 300 ramps per square mile, and those 27 zones cover 8.95 square miles, 30.7 percent of the HIGH band's area. Seventy-two of the 191 HIGH zones sit below the citywide median zone density of 582.1 per square mile. The lowest-density HIGH zone (OBJECTID 133, score 8) holds 52 ramps over 1.005 square miles, 51.8 per square mile. No HIGH zone has zero ramps. Second, the LOW band's 296.1 is itself dragged by two score-1 zones with zero ramps covering 34.58 square miles between them (OBJECTIDs 475 and 476, 30.36 and 4.22 square miles; they are the only two zero-ramp zones among the 479). Excluding those two zones, LOW density is 447.6 per square mile (30,244 ramps over 67.57 square miles), still below both MEDIUM and HIGH.

What this density measure is: ramps per square mile of zone land area. What it is not: ramps per street mile, per intersection, or per crossing. Zones differ in how much of their land is streets at all, and neither source layer publishes a street-mile or intersection denominator. The "What this might mean" section prints the alternative explanation this creates.

## Finding 6: Pedestrian crashes concentrate where the index says demand is high

Joining Denver crash reports (January 1, 2021 to October 15, 2026; 106,121 reports in the panel, 101,770 of them with coordinates, 100,085 of those inside a PDI zone) to the same zones measures whether the demand proxy tracks where pedestrian crashes in fact occur. Without this join, the crash data cannot be read against estimated walking demand at all, and the demand layer cannot be checked against any observed outcome.

Pedestrian-involved crashes by band (2,558 of the panel's 2,666 pedestrian crashes fall inside a PDI zone; 2,602 are geocoded):

- LOW: 788 pedestrian crashes of 40,275 crashes in the band (2.0 percent); 7.7 pedestrian crashes per square mile over the window.
- MEDIUM: 542 of 20,398 (2.7 percent); 23.4 per square mile.
- HIGH: 1,228 of 39,412 (3.1 percent); 42.2 per square mile.

Both measures rise with the band, and the per-square-mile count rises faster than all crashes per square mile (394.3 in LOW, 882.0 in MEDIUM, 1,353.4 in HIGH). By individual score, the pedestrian share of crashes is lowest at score 1 (1.4 percent, 211 of 14,628) and highest at scores 9 and 10 (4.0 percent, 378 of 9,514; and 4.4 percent, 110 of 2,473).

The comparison group built into this package: non-pedestrian crashes distribute differently across the same zones. Of crashes inside PDI zones, 48.0 percent of pedestrian crashes (1,228 of 2,558) fall in HIGH zones, against 39.2 percent of non-pedestrian crashes. The demand index separates pedestrian crash geography from general crash geography in the direction its construction predicts.

Severity within pedestrian crashes does not rise with the band in this window: the share of pedestrian crashes that are KSI crashes (a crash with a death or a serious injury, crash-count basis) is 5.6 percent in LOW (44 of 788), 4.1 percent in MEDIUM (22 of 542), and 4.3 percent in HIGH (53 of 1,228). The MEDIUM count of 22 is under the 30-case line in this project's methods and is flagged wherever it appears; no severity comparison in this brief rests on it alone.

Exposure caveat, stated prominently because it governs what Finding 6 can mean: the PDI is a proxy built from population and employment density, not a count of people walking, and this brief has no measured pedestrian-volume denominator. The figures above are shares of crashes and counts per square mile of land, not rates per pedestrian or per trip, and they support no causal reading in either direction. They show the index tracks where pedestrian crashes concentrate; they do not show that ramp density, or any other single factor, changes that concentration.

## Finding 7: Most pedestrian crashes happen within 150 feet of a ramp point

The nearest ramp point to each geocoded pedestrian crash (2,602 crashes; distances in State Plane feet against the full 72,770-point ramp layer) sits at a median distance of 50.6 feet. 1,282 of the 2,602 crashes (49.3 percent) are within 50 feet of a ramp point, 2,098 (80.6 percent) are within 150 feet, and 2,402 (92.3 percent) are within 300 feet; the 90th percentile distance is 273.4 feet. Within-150-feet shares by band of the crash location: HIGH 86.2 percent, MEDIUM 78.2 percent, LOW 73.2 percent.

This is a proximity measure only. Ramps delineated from imagery sit at street corners, and pedestrian crashes are reported at intersections and block locations, so the two point sets concentrate in the same places by construction. Proximity to a ramp point says nothing about the condition or accessibility of that ramp (Finding 1's caveat), nothing about whether a crossing was available on the corner where a crash occurred, and nothing about causation in either direction.

## Finding 8: Every neighborhood holds ramps; density is highest in the inner north neighborhoods

The neighborhood assignment (61,999 ramps placed) finds at least one ramp in every one of the 78 statistical neighborhoods, with a median neighborhood density of 556.2 ramps per square mile. The largest counts are Central Park (5,814 ramps), Gateway - Green Valley Ranch (4,589), and Montbello (2,056), which are also among the largest neighborhoods by area. The highest densities are Whittier (1,207.6 per square mile, 680 ramps), City Park West (1,192.6, 638 ramps), North Capitol Hill (1,100.5, 388), Cole (1,099.4, 563), and Congress Park (1,069.7, 1,101). The lowest is DIA (16.5 per square mile, 699 ramps over 42.36 square miles, most of it airfield), followed by Kennedy (47.0, 33 ramps). The full per-neighborhood panel is published with this brief (data/neighborhood_panel.csv), and the per-zone panel behind Findings 5 and 6 is published as data/taz_panel.csv.

## What this might mean

One reading of Finding 5 is that ramp supply, measured against land area, has not concentrated where the city's own index estimates the most walking: the area-weighted density in HIGH zones (563.3 per square mile) sits below the MEDIUM band (663.1), and 27 HIGH zones covering nearly a third of the HIGH band's land fall below 300 per square mile. On that reading, the zones listed in the published panel (the lowest-density HIGH zones are named in the package's stats file) are the specific places a field check would start, because a ramp point there is sparse relative to both the land area and the estimated demand.

The strongest alternative explanation is that land area is the wrong denominator for part of this gap. Ramp counts track intersections, and intersections track street-grid density: a HIGH zone that contains a campus, a rail yard, a large park, or large-format parcels can carry high population-plus-employment density with few street corners per square mile, while a MEDIUM residential grid carries corners every block. On that reading, some or all of the HIGH band's lower area-weighted density is a street-pattern artifact rather than a supply gap, and the median-zone figures in Finding 5 (HIGH highest at 714.0) are consistent with it. The deciding evidence would be ramps per intersection or per street mile by zone, and neither published layer carries that denominator. This brief reports the area-normalized figures with that limit attached and makes no claim about which reading explains more of the gap.

On Finding 6, the alternative explanation is printed with the finding: population and employment density raise both the index and the number of people exposed to traffic, so pedestrian crashes concentrating in HIGH zones is expected on exposure alone and requires no infrastructure story in either direction.

## Who can act

- **Denver Department of Transportation and Infrastructure (DOTI)** controls the sidewalk and curb ramp program and is the publisher-side owner of both layers' subject matter. Concrete action: field-verify ramp presence, condition, and ADA accessibility in the low-density HIGH zones this brief's panel identifies (starting with the 27 HIGH zones below 300 ramps per square mile, 8.95 square miles), and publish the verified result by zone. Cost class: budget decision (a field verification program across 27 zones is a staffed program, not a data edit). Outcome measure: a published zone-level verification table that can be joined to data/taz_panel.csv in this package, and a ramp layer whose next pull carries a condition or accessibility attribute populated for a stated share of points, replacing the current state (COMMENTS null on 72,770 of 72,770 records).
- **Denver's open data program (Technology Services / DenverGIS)**, publisher of both layers, controls what the published record can support. Concrete action: add a street-mile or intersection-count denominator by Traffic Analysis Zone to the published demand-index package, so ramp supply can be normalized per intersection rather than per square mile of land. Cost class: low-cost operational change (a derived field on an existing layer). Outcome measure: the next pull of ODC_ADMN_PEDDEMANDINDEX_A or a companion layer carries the denominator, and this brief's Finding 5 can be rerun on the per-intersection basis its caveat names.
- **Denver City Council**, in its budget process, controls DOTI's program funding. Concrete action: where ramp-program funding is weighed, weigh it against a zone baseline - ramps per square mile by demand band (HIGH 563.3, MEDIUM 663.1, LOW 296.1 in this brief) plus the list of low-density HIGH zones - and ask DOTI for the per-intersection version of the same baseline. Cost class: budget decision. Outcome measure: a baseline tabled in the budget process that this package's panels can be checked against, and movement in the HIGH band's area-weighted density on a later pull.

## Data notes

- Snapshot and vintage: ramp figures are from the pull of October 28, 2026 (72,770 records; server count and local count equal). The ramp service data was last edited July 12, 2024, and its points were delineated from imagery through the 2022 capture cycle, so the layer is a 2022-imagery snapshot served in 2026: ramps built, removed, or rebuilt since the imagery are not reflected. PDI figures are from the same day's pull (479 zones); that service reports its data last edited August 7, 2026.
- Accessibility caveat: the ramp layer's service description states ramps do not have to be ADA accessible to be captured and imagery cannot discern accessibility. A ramp point is a location only, everywhere in this package.
- CREATEDATE handling: ArcGIS date fields arrive as epoch milliseconds. Null values, and non-null values at or before the epoch or before 1950 or after the service edit date, are mapped to unknown and excluded from any date denominator; in this pull there were no null or sentinel values - all 72,770 CREATEDATE values are valid delineation dates in 2015-2024. CREATEDATE is never used as a construction date or ramp age (Finding 2).
- PDI definitions: score and ranking are the layer's own fields. The ranking bands (LOW 1-4, MEDIUM 5-6, HIGH 7-10) were verified by a server-side group-by on both fields, not assumed. PDI_Score is populated on all 479 zones; no zone is excluded from a band denominator. One PDI polygon (OBJECTID 258, score 8) is stored with an invalid geometry; its own area differs by 3.0 percent under a validity repair (0.114 against 0.111 square miles), which moves the HIGH band density by less than 0.1 ramps per square mile, and its ramp count (19) is retained and flagged in the package's reviewer notes.
- Areas: zone and neighborhood areas are computed by projecting polygons from EPSG:4326 to EPSG:2877 (Colorado Central State Plane, feet) and dividing square feet by 27,878,400. The PDI total computed this way (154.39 square miles) matches the sum of the layer's own Shape__Area field (154.39 square miles). Neighborhood polygons in the warehouse stack are stored in EPSG:4326 (verified from coordinate ranges), and all point-in-polygon work was run in lon/lat.
- Joins: point-in-polygon, first matching polygon; no ramp or crash point matched more than one polygon in any join in this package. Match rates: ramps to PDI zones 61,983 of 72,770 (85.2 percent, flagged - Finding 4 gives the systematic explanation); ramps to neighborhoods 61,999 of 72,770 (85.2 percent); crashes to PDI zones 100,085 of 101,770 geocoded reports (98.3 percent). The 4,351 crash reports without coordinates (4.1 percent of the panel) are excluded from the spatial joins only and remain in the panel total; 64 of the panel's 2,666 pedestrian crashes are among them.
- Crash definitions: the warehouse traffic accidents panel (built from Denver crash reports), reported date in America/Denver on or after January 1, 2021, deduplicated on offense ID (0 duplicates in the panel), through October 15, 2026 (2026 is a partial year). A pedestrian crash is a crash whose pedestrian_involved flag is set. A KSI crash is a crash with a death or a serious injury on the panel's crash-count basis (ksi_crash_count_basis). No traffic-volume or pedestrian-volume denominator exists in the sources; Finding 6's caveat is part of the finding.
- Layering test: the PDI layer is what lets this brief compare ramp supply against estimated demand (Finding 5); the crash layer is what lets it check the demand proxy against an observed outcome and add the comparison group (Finding 6); the neighborhood layer is what lets it cross-check the city-boundary explanation for unmatched ramps and report named places (Findings 4 and 8). Each layer's claim is stated with its finding.
- Reproduction: code/pull_data.py records the full pulls and server counters; code/build_stats.py builds stats.json and both panels; code/audit_section10.py records the pre-flight audit (output in data/audit_output.txt); code/verify_independent.py is the independent recomputation behind verification-ledger-independent.md; code/charts.py draws the charts.
